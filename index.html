About AI-CT Teacher

AI-CT Teacher is a web-based practice space for English teacher education. It is built for students who are preparing to become English language teachers, and for the teacher educators who guide them.

What is AI-CT Teacher?

AI-CT stands for Artificial Intelligence – Critical Thinking. The platform presents realistic classroom problems from English language teaching, such as students who will not speak, grammar that is memorised but not used, or a mixed-ability class with one coursebook text. Students work through each problem in seven ordered stages. Artificial intelligence takes part in only one of them.

The aim is not to produce a quick answer. The aim is to make a future teacher's own thinking visible, structured and open to development, while learning how to work with AI-assisted learning tools without simply following them.

Purpose and audience

Purpose: to support the development of critical-thinking skills among future English teachers through structured AI-supported learning activities.

Designed for: students in English teacher education and teacher training programmes, trainee and early-career teachers of English, and lecturers or mentors who want a structured way to discuss AI in English language teaching.

Status: a research-oriented prototype. See what the platform does and does not claim.

Why critical thinking matters for future English teachers

Teaching English involves constant professional judgment. A teacher decides how to correct errors, how to assess speaking fairly, how to adapt one text for learners of different levels, and how to keep a class of thirty on task. Few of these problems have a single correct answer, so good decisions depend on analysing the situation, weighing evidence and considering alternatives.

Critical thinking skills also matter because teachers model them in front of learners. Learners notice whether their teacher questions a claim, asks for evidence, or accepts whatever a textbook, a colleague or a website says.

How artificial intelligence can support critical thinking

Future teachers are already meeting AI-generated lesson ideas, activities and explanations. These can be helpful, but they can also be fluent, generic, poorly matched to a class, or wrong, including invented statistics and sources.

AI-CT Teacher turns this into a learning opportunity. An AI response becomes a case to be analysed: what does it claim, what does it assume, what is missing, and can its claims be checked? The design idea is that practising this kind of evaluation, after thinking independently first, helps future teachers use AI critically. This is the design rationale of the platform; its effectiveness still has to be tested in classroom research.

How it works

Each learning session follows the same four steps, so students always know where they are, what to do, why, and what comes next.

  1. 1

    Choose a classroom task

    Pick one of eight English-teaching situations from the task library, from speaking and grammar to assessment and classroom management.

  2. 2

    Work through the seven stages

    Analyse the problem yourself, write your own prompt to AI, then critique, check and challenge the response. The stages must be completed in order.

  3. 3

    Write your own conclusion

    State a claim with reasoning, evidence and a conclusion in your own words, then reflect on how your thinking changed.

  4. 4

    See a transparent profile

    A six-component rubric shows exactly which indicators were found in your record, and where further development may be useful.

The AI-CT 7C Cycle

The AI-CT 7C Cycle is the learning process at the centre of the platform. Seven stages always appear in the same order, and later stages open only after the earlier ones are complete.

  1. 1

    CONTEXT

    Read a realistic classroom situation and analyse it on your own, with no AI. Name the main problem, possible causes, the information you would need, and what you would do first. This becomes your baseline.

  2. 2

    CONSULT the only stage with AI

    Write your own prompt and ask AI for possible solutions. The response is shown as material to examine. The platform does not tell you whether it is correct.

  3. 3

    CRITIQUE

    Analyse the response: its main claim, assumptions, convincing and questionable parts, weaknesses, omissions, and whether it fits this specific classroom. A position needs reasons, not just "I agree" or "I disagree".

  4. 4

    CHECK

    Choose the claims that need verifying, find sources yourself, and record the claim, source, evidence and your verdict: supported, partially supported, unsupported or uncertain. The platform does not verify sources for you.

  5. 5

    CHALLENGE

    Argue back. Propose at least one alternative solution, say what you would change, name the disadvantages of the AI suggestion, write a counterargument, and test your thinking against changed classroom conditions.

  6. 6

    CONCLUDE

    Write an independent professional judgment: claim, reasoning, evidence and conclusion. It is compared with your first response from the Context stage, and a check discourages copying the AI text.

  7. 7

    REFLECT

    Look back at your thinking: what you believed before consulting AI, what AI changed or challenged, what you rejected, which evidence mattered, and how confident you are, on a scale of 1 to 5.

Six of the seven stages are your own thinking. Artificial intelligence appears once, and always as something to be analysed rather than followed.

How can future English teachers evaluate AI-generated responses?

The platform trains a repeatable habit. Before using any AI suggestion in a lesson, a future teacher can ask:

  • Claims: what exactly is being asserted, and which claims would I need to verify?
  • Assumptions: what must be true about the learners, the school or the context for this advice to work?
  • Evidence: is there a source I can find myself? Could a statistic, quotation or reference be invented?
  • Fit: does it suit this class size, level, age, time limit and set of materials?
  • Gaps and conflicts: what is missing, and do any parts of the response contradict each other?
  • Alternatives: what else could I do, and what are the costs of each option?

Where to practise it

In the AI-CT Teacher app, students tag each part of an AI response as convincing, questionable or "check this". Parts marked for checking move into an evidence table, where the student records a source and a verdict. This makes the evaluation process visible and easy to discuss with a mentor or lecturer.

Because the demonstration responses are written with deliberate weaknesses, students can compare their own critique with what the response was designed to test, after they have finished their analysis.

Features

Everything is designed for English language education, not as a generic thinking-skills tool.

Structured 7C workflow

Progress indicator, required checklists, Back, Next, Save and Continue later. Essential stages cannot be skipped by accident.

Student thinks first

AI is unavailable until the student has written an initial analysis, which is kept for comparison at the end.

Write your own prompt

Students compose the request to AI themselves, with prompts to consider context, constraints and the kind of answer wanted.

Evidence-checking table

Claim, source, source type, evidence and verdict, with an honest note that the platform does not verify sources.

Transparent assessment

Six components scored 1 to 4 from visible indicators, with total, maximum, percentage and a performance profile.

Progress and reflection history

Charts across completed tasks, confidence before and after, and a reflection history with follow-up notes.

Structured records

Every completed task yields a record that can be exported as JSON or CSV for teaching or research piloting.

Teacher dashboard prototype

A cohort view with completion, average performance and component profiles, shown with clearly labelled demo data.

Responsive and private by design

Works on desktop, tablet and mobile. Records stay in your own browser and are not sent to a server.

Assessment

The assessment page turns each completed task into a simple, transparent profile. Each of six working critical-thinking components is scored from 1 (Beginning) to 4 (Advanced).

Analysis

Breaking a problem or response into parts.

Evaluation

Judging strengths and weaknesses with reasons.

Inference

Drawing conclusions that follow from checked evidence.

Argumentation

Building a connected case with a counterargument in view.

Alternative Thinking

Generating and testing other options.

Reflection

Explaining how thinking changed and how sure you are.

Each score is based on three predefined indicators that are shown next to it, so students can see exactly why a level was given. They can also rate themselves and compare. The result is worded as a profile, for example stronger performance in some components and areas where further development may be useful, and never as a verdict about a person.

Important: the indicators check whether elements of critical thinking are present in a student's record. They cannot judge the quality of ideas. Scores are provisional and are not a validated measure; for research, a trained human rater should score records with the same rubric.

Student progress

Students can follow their work across tasks: completed records, a radar chart of average component levels, scores across records, and confidence at the start and end of each task. The reflection history collects every reflection in one place and allows follow-up notes, for example after trying an idea in a real classroom.

What the platform does and does not claim

AI-CT Teacher is an electronic methodological prototype developed within a research project on English teacher education. Each completed task produces a structured record: the task, the student's initial response, the AI interaction, the critical analysis, the evidence checking, the alternative solution, the final conclusion, the reflection and the assessment profile.

It does not claim that the platform has been proven to improve critical thinking. Whether it does has to be established later through pedagogical experimental research. The six components are working assessment dimensions of the prototype, not scientifically validated by the platform itself.

About AI responses: on this website the AI responses are pre-written demonstration responses, clearly labelled as such, unless a live AI connection is available in the environment where the app is opened.

Frequently asked questions

Who is AI-CT Teacher for?

It is for future English teachers in teacher education and teacher training, and for lecturers and mentors who support them. All tasks come from English language classrooms.

Does the platform give me the answers?

No. The platform is designed so that you think first, treat the AI response as something to analyse and verify, and finish with your own conclusion. It does not tell you whether the AI response is correct.

Where is my work stored?

In your own browser on your own device. There are no accounts and no server database in this prototype, so clearing your browser data removes your records. You can export completed records as JSON or CSV.

Does it use real artificial intelligence?

The app can use a live AI connection where the environment provides one. Otherwise it shows a pre-written demonstration response, always labelled "AI RESPONSE — DEMONSTRATION". Demonstration responses do not react to your prompt.

Is AI-CT Teacher proven to improve critical thinking?

No. It is a research-oriented prototype. Its effectiveness has not yet been tested and would need classroom research.

Does it work on a phone or tablet?

Yes. The site and the app are responsive and work on desktop, tablet and mobile browsers.
